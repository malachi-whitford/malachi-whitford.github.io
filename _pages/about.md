I'm Malachi Whitford, an ecologist and tenured Professor of Environmental Science and Biology at Clovis Community College in Fresno, California. I study how behavior, physical performance, and environmental conditions together determine the outcomes of ecological interactions, with a focus on predator-prey interactions between reptiles and small mammals. As the college's sole Environmental Science faculty member, I built its Environmental Science Program from the ground up and run a field-based teaching and research program that puts students to work on real ecological questions.

### Research
I'm drawn to interactions that unfold in a fraction of a second, where tiny differences in individual performance decide who survives. My doctoral work at San Diego State University and UC Davis focused on free-ranging sidewinder rattlesnakes and desert kangaroo rats. Rattlesnakes are sit-and-wait ambush predators, so I could predict where encounters would happen and record them under natural conditions with portable video surveillance units, geophones, and field high-speed videography.

That work showed that predator and prey are best studied together, in the field. Kangaroo rat sand-kicking displays can cause rattlesnakes to abandon their ambush sites entirely. Kangaroo rats can respond to an attack in under 20 milliseconds, close to the limits of vertebrate reaction time. A bite isn't always the end, either: kangaroo rats sometimes kick a snake's head to dislodge its fangs. I also found that temperature strongly affects rattlesnake defensive strikes but has a weaker effect on predatory strikes. This research has been featured by the *New York Times*, *National Geographic*, the *Washington Post*, the BBC, and PBS Deep Look.

My current and future research builds on this foundation in three directions:

- **Escape performance in changing habitats:** how invasive grasses and substrate change kangaroo rat escape behavior and performance, with implications for imperiled kangaroo rat species in invaded landscapes.
- **Snake-bat interactions:** how predators exploit bats during cave emergences, comparing Puerto Rican boas (*Chilabothrus inornatus*) in Puerto Rico with coachwhips (*Masticophis flagellum*) at a Texas cave I'm working to establish as a study site.
- **Sciurid niche partitioning:** how sympatric chipmunk species in the Sierra Nevada divide up habitat, and how body size, behavior, kinematics, and diet reinforce those patterns.

### Undergraduate Research: The NATURE Program
I founded the NATURE (Novel Approaches to Terrestrial Undergraduate Research in Ecology) Program to bring community college students into the full research process. I treat students much like graduate students: they develop questions, collect and analyze data, present at conferences, and publish. Since 2023, I have mentored 13 undergraduate researchers. Four of them are co-authors on a 2026 paper in *Western Wildlife*.

Most of our projects grew out of observations in my field courses. Current projects include:

- Modeling the habitat requirements and potential spread of invasive squamates in California
- Modeling the distribution of California pipevine (*Aristolochia californica*) under climate change
- Testing how gray fox digestion affects coffeeberry (*Frangula californica*) germination
- Describing predator-deterrent signaling in coarse-haired pocket mice (*Chaetodipus* sp.)

### Teaching
I believe students learn biology by engaging with the living world, and that every student can master difficult material with the right scaffolding. I developed two field courses, BIOL 7: Field Biology and BIOL 17: Wildlife and Plant Conservation Field Methods, which together with my other courses add up to more than 45 field trips a year. In Field Biology, students learn to identify and describe more than 500 local species. In Field Methods, they survey real sites and write professional biological technical reports like the ones required for environmental review.

I also designed the curriculum for Environmental Science, Plant Science, and Soil Science. I created the Environmental Science and Plant Science AS-T degrees and certificates in Plant Science and Wildlife and Plant Conservation Field Methods. Every course in the program has zero textbook costs, supported in part by a $25,000 ASCCC OERI grant I co-led. College vans make sure transportation never keeps a student out of the field. Since 2022, enrollment in the Environmental Science program has grown thirtyfold.

Outside the classroom, I co-founded the Conservation Club with my students and serve as its advisor. The club has created a campus native plant garden and run trips to Pinnacles, Sequoia and Kings Canyon, Morro Bay, and the Monterey Bay Aquarium. I'm also a natural history docent for the Sierra Foothill Conservancy.

### Background
I started college at Fresno City College, transferred to Humboldt State University for my B.S. in Biology, and earned my Ph.D. in Ecology through the joint doctoral program at San Diego State University and UC Davis. Before joining Clovis Community College, I worked as a staff scientist and project manager at Colibri Ecological Consulting. There I conducted rare plant and wildlife surveys, water resource assessments, and environmental impact assessments. I also served as scientific advisor for the documentary *Life of Sound: Episode 1 – The Desert*.

### Tools for Research and Teaching
I build many of the tools my students use. These include a Google Drive trail camera review app that codes and organizes thousands of images automatically, a behavior coding app for video analysis, species identification study tools, and interactive simulations for my courses. These tools let students take part in research with nothing more than an internet connection. I also build species distribution models with MaxEnt in R and use 3D printing to make custom field equipment.
